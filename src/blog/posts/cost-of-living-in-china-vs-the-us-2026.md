## **A glance at the answer**

<p class="lead-quote">Yes, China is much cheaper in dollars. Essential bills for one adult run about $540 a month in China vs $2,300 in the US.
But pay is lower too, so the real answer depends on who signs your paycheck. An urban Chinese worker on the official average wage keeps 59% of take-home pay after bills. The average American keeps 50%, or 36% if they drive.</p>

**How we compared:** 

* One adult, full-time job, renting a 1-bedroom alone. 
* Take-home pay is after tax and payroll deductions. 
* Prices are national averages, converted at the ECB rate on 19 October 2026 (1 USD = 6.68 yuan). 
* Health insurance only counts when you pay it on top of your salary.

## **How much do people earn in China vs the US?**

The average American takes home about **3.5 times more** than the average urban Chinese worker. And China has two very different "average" salaries.

![Take home pay](/blog/images/52.2.png)

China's statistics bureau reports wages separately for "non-private units" (state-owned companies, government and larger firms) and private companies. The headline figure most news sites quote is the first one. Private-sector workers earn about 45% less.

Two more things cut into a Chinese paycheck:

* **Deductions:** 10.5% goes to pension, medical and unemployment insurance. Another 5-12% goes to a housing fund, a forced savings account you can only use for buying or renting a home.
* **Longer weeks:** Chinese company employees average **48.2 hours a week**, so hourly pay is even lower than the monthly gap suggests.

Location matters too. Workers in eastern China earn about 46% more than workers in central China.

## **How much is rent in China vs the US?**

A 1-bedroom outside the city center costs **$1,339 in the US and $236 in China**. As a share of pay, China wins, unless you work in the private sector.

![How much rent does it cost?](/blog/images/52.3.png)

* Shanghai is the outlier. Its rent per square meter is about **2.5 times the average of 50 major Chinese cities**, and it rose six months in a row in 2026 while the national average fell. That’s why a shared room (2,000-2,500 yuan, or $300-375) is the norm for young workers there.
* The US has its own squeeze. **49% of American renter households** now spend more than 30% of their income on housing, a record high. If you're wondering what a healthy share looks like, the 50/30/20 rule caps all your needs, rent included, at 50% of take-home pay.

## **How much are monthly bills in China vs the US?**

Utilities, phone and internet cost about **$70 a month in China vs $350 in the US**. China wins almost every line, with one surprise.

![Monthly bills in China vs the US](/blog/images/52.4.png)

* **Gas is the one thing that costs more in China.** 92-octane petrol in Shanghai is 8.57 yuan a liter, which works out to about $4.86 a gallon.
* **Owning a car in Shanghai starts with an auction.** The average winning bid for a license plate was 94,368 yuan ($14,100) in May 2026, and only 11.5% of bidders got one. That's before you buy the actual car.
* **American drivers pay for the privilege too.** Only 8% of US households go without a car, and the average used-car payment plus full insurance comes to $739 a month. That's more than half of average rent, before a single gallon of gas. If you're weighing that bill, see whether an electric car is worth it.
* **Healthcare hides costs in different places.** American employers quietly pay about 85% of the premium, and you still face a $1,886 deductible before most coverage starts. In Shanghai, the outpatient deductible is 500 yuan ($75) a year, then insurance covers 70-80%. But Chinese patients still pay about 27% of all health spending out of their own pockets.

## **How much do groceries cost in China vs the US?**

Groceries are much cheaper in China in dollars. Measured in minutes of work, they cost more on almost everything.

*Milk takes 2 minutes of work in the US and 17 in China. Rice is the only item where China comes out ahead.*

![Grocery bills](/blog/images/52.5.png)


Two prices are moving fast in China right now:

* **Pork** is cheap. Live hog prices fell to their lowest level since 2019 this year.
* **Eggs** went the other way, up 14% in a year (September 2026).

## **What's left after the bills?**

The US keeps the most dollars. China's urban average worker keeps the biggest share. China's private-sector worker keeps the least.

 *An urban Chinese worker on the average wage keeps 59% of take-home pay. A private-sector worker keeps 27%.*

![What's left after the bills?](/blog/images/52.6.png)

## **What about everything else?**

<div class="callout callout-note"><span class="callout-label">The table above only covers essentials.</span><p>Eating out, coffee, clothes and the gym all come out of that “left over” money, and this is where China and the US split in a surprising way.</p></div>

![](/blog/images/52.7.png)

Local is cheap, global is expensive. A basic Chinese restaurant meal costs less work time than a cheap meal in the US. But anything with a global price tag (coffee, fast food, brands, the gym) costs about the same in dollars in both countries. On a Chinese paycheck, that means two to four times more hours of work.

## **So how much do people actually save?**

“Left over” isn’t the same as “saved.” Here’s what people really keep:

* US: about 4% of income (BEA, August 2026). One-person households spend about $4,070 a month in total, close to 90% of average take-home pay.
* Urban China: about 36.5%. Urban residents spend 35,869 yuan a year out of 56,502 yuan of disposable income (NBS, 2025). Household deposits grew nearly 10% in 2025.

These are national averages, including homeowners and retirees, and money put toward buying a home counts as savings. A young renter in Shanghai likely saves less. Still, China’s real advantage isn’t just cheaper bills. Chinese households spend far less of what’s left.

![The scorecard](/blog/images/52.8.png)



<div class="verdict-card"><span class="verdict-label">Our verdict</span><p><strong>China is cheaper than the US on almost everything you pay for in dollars, except gas and owning a car.</strong> Measured against local pay, it comes down to your employer. A worker at a Chinese state-owned or large company keeps more of their paycheck than the average American. A private-sector worker keeps about a quarter, and pays more work time for food.
If you're moving to China on a foreign salary, it's a bargain. If you're earning local private-sector pay, it's tighter than the price tags suggest.</p></div>
