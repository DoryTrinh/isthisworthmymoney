## **A glance at the answer**

Saving 20% is out of reach on most low incomes. Start with 1% to 5% of take home pay and build a $1,000 starter buffer (or the local equivalent) first. 

> $83.33 a month gets you there in 12 months: 3.45% of take home pay for our US example. 
>
> At 5% it takes 8.3 months; at 1%, 41.4 months. 

Move the money into a separate account on payday, and take free money from a matched scheme if your country has one. 

> If costs already take all of your pay, stretch the timeline: $1,000 over 24 months is $41.67 a month.

### **How we compared**

* Two example earners, both full time: a US worker at the 10th percentile of weekly pay ($642 a week, 51.3% of the median, BLS Q2 2026) and a UK worker on the National Living Wage (£12.71 an hour from 1 April 2026 for age 21 and over, 37.5 hours a week).
* Measure: take home pay after income tax and social contributions. US: $2,417.00 a month (federal tax and FICA only, no state tax, 401(k) or health premiums). UK: £1,780.37 a month (England, Wales and Northern Ireland rates, no pension or student loan).
* Saving is shown as a share of take home pay, before interest, so the same percentages work in any currency.
* Survey data: Fed SHED 2025 (survey of 2025, published May 2026), Eurostat median saving rates by income fifth (2015, experimental statistics) and the FCA Financial Lives survey (May 2024 fieldwork).

## **What counts as a low income?**

> There's no single number. Most official definitions measure low income as a share of the median in your own country, so a low income in Germany and a low income in Bulgaria are very different amounts.

| Measure                      | Threshold                                                | Example                                                                                                   | Source                |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------- |
| Relative poverty (OECD)      | Below 50% of national median household disposable income | OECD average poverty rate 11.7% (2016)                                                                    | OECD                  |
| At risk of poverty (EU)      | Below 60% of national median disposable income           | One adult, EU 2024: about €1,079 a month; Germany €1,381, Spain €965, Bulgaria €391                       | Eurostat via Euronews |
| Low pay (OECD convention)    | Below two thirds of median pay                           | UK 2024: £11.39 an hour (hourly pay); US Q2 2026: $834 a week (computed from median full time weekly pay) | ONS; BLS              |
| Extreme poverty (World Bank) | $3.00 per person per day, 2021 PPP                       | 10.5% of the world (about 838 million people) in 2022                                                     | World Bank            |

**The simple test:** if your household income is below 50% or 60% of the median in your country, or your pay is below two thirds of median pay, you're on a low income by the standard definitions.

* Our US example earns $642 a week, 51.3% of the US median and below the $834 low pay line.
* Our UK example is on the legal wage floor for age 21 and over. £12.71 an hour (2026) is above the 2024 low pay line of £11.39, so we call it "on the minimum wage" rather than low paid.
* The UK line uses hourly pay and the US line uses weekly full time pay, so don't compare the two lines with each other.

## **How much can you realistically save on a low income?**

Not much at first. In the US, only 20% of adults with family income under $25,000 often or always have money left at the end of the month, and 21% have three months of expenses saved.

| Family income (US) | Doing at least okay financially | Often or always has money left at month end | Has 3 months of expenses saved |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Under $25,000      | 45%                             | 20%                                         | 21%                            |
| $25,000 to $49,999 | 55%                             | 26%                                         | 39%                            |
| $50,000 to $99,999 | 74%                             | 38%                                         | 55%                            |
| $100,000 or more   | 91%                             | 59%                                         | 75%                            |
| All adults         | 73%                             | 41%                                         | 55%                            |

*Source: Fed SHED 2025, Tables 1, 18 and 27, % of US adults.*
**Europe shows the same pattern in a different measure**: the median saving rate of each income fifth, as a share of disposable income (what's left after tax and benefits).

| Income fifth (euro area) | Median saving rate, 2015 | 2020       |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------ | ---------- |
| Poorest fifth            | minus 7.2%               | minus 3.4% |
| Second fifth             | 11.8%                    | 17.5%      |
| Middle fifth             | 20.4%                    | 25.9%      |
| Fourth fifth             | 28.4%                    | 33.8%      |
| Richest fifth            | 39.0%                    | 44.1%      |

*Source: Eurostat icw_sr_03, % of disposable income, experimental statistics.*

* The poorest fifth had a median saving rate of minus 7.2% in 2015. The median household in that group spent more than its disposable income, drawing on savings, credit or family help. The richest fifth saved 39.0%.
* 2020 rates are higher for every group because the pandemic cut what people could spend. Treat 2015 as the normal year.
* In the UK, 1 in 10 adults have no cash savings at all, and 21% have less than £1,000 to draw on in an emergency (FCA, May 2024 fieldwork, no income split).
* National averages hide the bottom. The US personal saving rate was 4.1% in August 2026 (BEA), an average across every income.

Our US example earns about $33,384 a year, which puts it in the $25,000 to $49,999 row: 26% often or always have money left over and 39% have three months saved. For the general benchmark, see [How much should you save per month?](https://isthisworthmymoney.com/blog/how-much-should-you-save/) This post sets a lower starting point.

**Why doesn't the 50/30/20 rule work on a low income?**

Because its 20% savings share is a big monthly number on a small income. [The 50/30/20 rule](https://isthisworthmymoney.com/blog/50-30-20-rule/) splits take home pay into 50% needs, 30% wants and 20% savings. The table puts that 20% next to 3% on our two examples.

| Per month                         | US example | UK example |
| --------------------------------- | ---------- | ---------- |
| Take home pay                     | $2,417.00  | £1,780.37  |
| 20% savings share (50/30/20 rule) | $483.40    | £356.07    |
| 3% of take home pay               | $72.51     | £53.41     |

$483.40 a month (US) is a lot to ask of an income band where only 26% often have money left at month end. Essentials also take a bigger share of spending at the bottom:

| Share of spending (US, 2024) | Lowest income fifth | All households |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------- | -------------- |
| Housing                      | about 42.0%         | 33.4%          |
| Food                         | about 15.6%         | 12.9%          |
| Transportation               | about 14.7%         | 17.0%          |
| Healthcare                   | about 9.7%          | 7.9%           |

*Source: BLS Consumer Expenditures 2024.* 

Lowest fifth shares are derived from BLS published changes, so read them as approximate.

* Housing and food take about 57.6% of spending in the lowest fifth, against 46.3% for all households. The "wants" share the rule asks you to cut from is smaller to begin with.
* Other countries show the same pattern. In Chile, Colombia, Finland, Israel and New Zealand, more than half of low income private renters spend over 40% of disposable income on housing (OECD). In the UK, poorer households spend a larger share on housing, fuel and power (ONS).

## **How much emergency money should you save first?**

Aim for $1,000 first, or the local equivalent. 39% of US adults couldn't cover a $1,000 expense from savings alone today.

| Emergency cost | US adults who could not cover it from savings alone |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| $100           | 18%                                                 |
| $500           | 30%                                                 |
| $1,000         | 39%                                                 |
| $2,000         | 50%                                                 |

*Source: computed from Fed SHED 2025, Table 26 (cumulative shares).*

* A smaller first step: $500 covers the $400 emergency the SHED survey asks about. 63% of US adults would pay a $400 expense with cash or its equivalent, and among adults with income under $50,000, 4 in 10 couldn't cover even $100 from savings.
* Without savings, a $400 bill goes elsewhere: 15% would put it on a credit card and pay over time, 10% would borrow from friends or family, 7% would sell something, 2% would use a payday loan or overdraft and 12% couldn't pay it right now (more than one answer allowed).
* Next stop, $2,000. In a Vanguard survey of its own investors, people with $2,000 or more saved reported 21% higher financial well being than those with none. Vanguard investors are wealthier than most low earners, so read it as a link between the two, not a promise.
* Keep saving $83.33 a month and the second $1,000 takes another 12 months: $2,000 in 24 months on our US example. For the full plan from $2,000 to three months of essential bills, see [How to Build an Emergency Fund From Scratch](https://isthisworthmymoney.com/blog/build-emergency-fund-from-scratch/).

### **Does saving small amounts make a difference?**

Yes. 1% of take home pay still adds up to $290 a year on the US example, and 5% reaches $1,000 in 8.3 months.

| Share of take home pay | US a month | US a year | Months to $1,000 | Months to $2,000 | UK a month | UK a year | Months to £1,000 |
| ---------------------- | ---------- | --------- | ---------------- | ---------------- | ---------- | --------- | ---------------- |
| 1%                     | $24.17     | $290      | 41.4             | 82.7             | £17.80     | £214      | 56.2             |
| 3%                     | $72.51     | $870      | 13.8             | 27.6             | £53.41     | £641      | 18.7             |
| 5%                     | $120.85    | $1,450    | 8.3              | 16.5             | £89.02     | £1,068    | 11.2             |
| 10%                    | $241.70    | $2,900    | 4.1              | 8.3              | £178.04    | £2,136    | 5.6              |

Before interest. The UK months are to £1,000, so don't compare them with the dollar months.

* The $83.33 a month target sits between the 3% and 5% rows: it's 3.45% of US example take home pay and reaches $1,000 in 12 months.
* Living somewhere else? Multiply your own monthly take home pay by the percentage. 3% of 1,500 a month is 45 a month, in any currency.
* For places to find the first few dollars, see [How to Save Money: 11 Weird Tricks That Actually Work](https://isthisworthmymoney.com/blog/how-to-save-money-11-tricks/).

## **How do you save money when there's nothing left over?**

Work out the gap first, then change the timeline or the goal before you give up on saving. Enter your take home pay and monthly costs below, and the calculator compares what you can save with what you need.

The calculator shows dollars, but the math works in any currency. Enter your own amounts and read the result in your own money.

How it works: what you CAN save is take home pay minus expenses minus a 5% buffer. What you NEED to save is the goal divided by the months. When CAN is lower than NEED, it offers three fixes.

| US example, $1,000 in 12 months                  | Per month                          |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| Take home pay                                    | $2,417.00                          |
| 5% buffer (calculator default)                   | $120.85                            |
| What you NEED to save ($1,000 / 12)              | $83.33                             |
| Highest monthly costs that still meet the target | $2,212.82 (91.6% of take home pay) |

If your costs are higher than that, one of the three fixes brings the monthly amount down:

| Fix                         | Example                                                     | Monthly saving needed                      |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Stretch the timeline        | $1,000 over 24 months                                       | $41.67 (1.72% of US example take home pay) |
| Shrink the goal             | $500 over 12 months                                         | $41.67                                     |
| Cut expenses proportionally | Trim every cost line by the same share until CAN meets NEED | Depends on your costs                      |

* Try 24 months first. It halves the monthly amount and keeps the $1,000 goal.
* Then try $500. It still covers the $400 emergency from the SHED survey.
* Cut costs last, and start with recurring bills. A [subscription audit](https://isthisworthmymoney.com/blog/subscription-audit/) is the quickest place to look, then food: see [eating out vs cooking at home](https://isthisworthmymoney.com/blog/eating-out-vs-cooking-at-home/). To track every line, [Best free budget calculators in 2026](https://isthisworthmymoney.com/blog/best-free-budget-calculators-in-2026/) lists free tools.

### **Where should you keep savings so you don't spend them?**

In a separate account from the one you spend from, with the transfer set for payday and the money reachable within a day or two.

* Mobile money counts. In low and middle income economies, 40% of adults saved formally (at a financial institution or in a mobile money account) in 2024, up 16 points since 2021 (Global Findex 2025).
* Before you open an account, check that your country's deposit guarantee covers it.

<div class="callout callout-tip"><span class="callout-label">Tip</span><p>Set the transfer for the day your pay arrives, not the end of the month. Money that never sits in your spending account is harder to spend.</p></div>

## **Are there government savings schemes for low income earners?**

Some countries add free money to what you save, so check whether yours does. The UK's Help to Save is one example of matched saving.

| Scheme          | Who                                       | Rules                                                                                                                              | Most you can get             |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| UK Help to Save | Certain people receiving Universal Credit | Pay in £1 to £50 each calendar month; 50p bonus for every £1 saved, paid at the end of years 2 and 4; account closes after 4 years | £2,400 paid in, £1,200 bonus |

*Source: gov.uk, Help to Save. Amounts in pounds (UK).*
The £50 monthly maximum is 2.81% of the UK example take home pay, inside the 1% to 5% range. Pay it in for all 48 months and the bonus adds £1,200 to your £2,400. Wherever you live, ask your government and your employer whether either one adds money to savings, and check the rules on the official site.

Set the transfer for payday, keep the money in a separate account and take any matched scheme you qualify for. If the calculator says the goal doesn't fit, stretch it to 24 months ($41.67 a month) before you give up. If your costs already take more than your pay, the first step is more income or support, because no saving rule fixes a pay gap. One emergency on the way resets the clock, and that's what the buffer is for. That's how to save money on a low income: a small amount, moved on payday.</p></div>
